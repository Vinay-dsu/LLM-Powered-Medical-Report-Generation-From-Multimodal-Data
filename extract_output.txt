Abstract—For hospitals and clinics around the world, the
analysis of medical images, particularly chest X-rays, is still a
significant hurdle. There is a pressing need for automated systems
that can help doctors produce accurate and well-structured
reports because there are not enough radiologists and an ever-
increasing number of images to analyze. For our research, we
designed a multimodal deep learning architecture that integrates
BioGPT, a biological language model, with a personalized Vision
Transformer (rad-dino, pre-trained specifically on radiological
images). We use a novel cross-attention fusion method to integrate
the two. In short, our system analyzes a chest X-ray image,
analyzes any patient data that has been provided, and generates
a comprehensive radiology report with impressions, findings, and
clinical recommendations. To implement this, we also utilized
Flask to build a complete web application that can export
professional PDFs right from the browser and conduct real-time
inference on a GPU. The tool was able to attain a 100% coherence
rate when tested on 50 unique X-rays from the IEEE-8023
COVID dataset. This implies that it was able to provide medically
accurate and properly formatted text for everything ranging from
COVID-19 or ARDS to healthy lungs. On a standard NVIDIA
GPU, processing time takes only about 8 seconds per image. Since
it is quite fast, it is possible to implement it in a clinical setting
without interfering with the workflow.
Index Terms—Medical Report Generation, Vision Transformer,
BioGPT, Multimodal Learning, Cross-Attention, Chest X-ray,
Large Language Model, Clinical Decision Support
I. INTRODUCTION
Chest X-rays contribute to almost 40% of the massive
number of diagnostic imaging exams conducted by hospitals
every year[1]. The issue is that each and every one of these
images needs to be carefully analyzed by a professional.
There is a current shortage of 2 million radiologists globally,
according to the World Health Organization, and this affects
developing regions the most. Patients have to wait longer to
get a diagnosis when there are too many tests and not enough
professionals to analyze them, which is a clear indicator that
their chances of having a positive outcome are lower.
Scientists applied conventional computer-aided diagnostic
(CAD) methods to attempt to solve the problem for a rather
long time. A pleural effusion, cardiomegaly, or pneumonia are
some of the simple yes/no questions that the vast majority of
the old systems try to solve [2]. While this kind of binary
classification is certainly useful, it completely disregards the
needs of physicians in the field. A real physician requires a
full radiological report that should contain suggestions about
what to do next, a diagnostic impression, and perspective on
the specific findings. A simple "positive for pneumonia" label
just cannot substitute a carefully composed text that should
take into account the real medical history of the patient, as
well as the visual information.
AI is beginning to make real headway in terms of actual
therapeutic applications[13],[14]. With huge breakthroughs in
large language models and vision transformers [12],[15], we
finally have a good foundation for automating the process of
writing complex medical reports and analyzing images on a
large scale. BioGPT[11], for instance, has become remarkably
skilled at writing coherent biomedical text, while specialized
vision transformers [3] are working to improve software for
the detection of medical anomalies. The hard part is getting
the text and vision sides to communicate with each other.
To fill this gap, we developed a new multi-modal frame-
work. Here are the things that this study has offered:
1) We developed a unique architecture that combines BioGPT
with rad-dino, a ViT encoder that is specifically adapted for
radiology. The learnable cross-attention mechanism allows
the text and image inputs to always refer to each other.
2) To ensure that the reports are consistent with the patient’s
specific medical context, the model can actually take into
account the patient’s clinical history whenever it is avail-
able.
3) Rather than ending the result in a notebook code, we pro-
vided a complete web-ready clinical assistance application.
This allows for the immediate export of the correct PDF
reports.
4) We tested the technology using fifty different clinical chest

X-rays. Our results showed that the reports generated by
the technology were always natural-sounding in reading
and medically coherent, reaching full coherence at 100
II. LITERATURE REVIEW
A. Medical Image Classification
But in the last few years, deep learning has completely
changed the way we evaluate medical images. It started to
take hold with Wang et al [1]. and ChestX-ray8, which was
a huge dataset, 100,000 X-rays, and it set the bar for all that
came after. Then came Rajpurkar [2] and CheXNet, which
could identify pneumonia as accurately as human radiologists.
Then came Irvin et al [4]. and CheXpert, which introduced
uncertainty labels to make the models a little more realistic.
But at the end of the day, these are still classification models.
They spit out a diagnosis, but they don’t provide the kind of
reasoning that doctors use.
B. Automated Report Generation
Jing and Xing [5] were among the first to try to generate
full reports in order to go beyond just labels. In order to have
the model consider both the image and the text history at
the same time, they used a co-attention method. However, the
state of the art has progressed rapidly since then. In order to
increase the quality of the generated text, the R2Gen model [6]
has recently used cross-modal memory networks and achieved
some remarkably high scores on standard evaluation metrics.
C. Vision Transformers in Medical Imaging
When the initial Vision Transformer (ViT) [3] came out,
it showed that with enough data, transformers could handle
image processing better than Convolutional Neural Networks
(CNNs). Then came DINO [7], showing that these models
are capable of learning very important visual features on their
own without the need for human annotation. This same idea
is applied straight to radiology with the Rad-dino model. The
embeddings learned by the model already know what to search
for in order to diagnose something like fluid accumulation or
an enlarged heart because it was trained on millions of X-rays.
D. Biomedical Language Models
While models such as GPT-3 are very good textually, they
do not really "speak" medicine. This is where BioGPT comes
in [11]. Although it has read over 15 million abstracts during
its training stage, BioGPT is based on the GPT-2 model. It is
thus the best model to use for writing medical reports because
it knows how to mix complex medical talk without getting
confused.
III. PROPOSED METHODOLOGY
A. System Overview
Our system is built upon a classic multimodal encoder-
decoder playbook with a few highly specialized components
thrown in. To obtain the spatial information, we start by
passing an X-ray of the chest through a visual encoder. At
the same time, we pass the patient’s clinical history through a
language model to obtain the text information. We then employ
a cross-attention mechanism to merge these two sources of
information. Once the combined information is ready, we pass
it back to the language model so that it can build a coherent
and readable report sentence by sentence. You can see how all
this is done in Fig. 1
Fig. 1. Proposed multimodal architecture for medical report generation. The
ViT encoder processes the X-ray image while BioGPT tokenizes the patient
history. Features are fused via cross-attention before autoregressive report
generation.
B. Visual Encoder: Radiology-Specific ViT
We decided to use Rad-dino as the "eyes" of the operation. It
is a Vision Transformer that applied the DINO self-supervised
learning approach to sift through massive datasets of radiolog-
ical images and learn to interpret X-ray images on its own. It
breaks down a regular image I ∈R224×224×3 into a collection
of small squares of 16 × 16 pixels when we provide it with the
image. This gives us 196 distinct "patches." These patches are
then processed through multiple layers of transformers with
multi-head self-attention (MHSA) in the ViT:
Zvis = ViT(I) ∈RN×dvit
(1)
where N = 197 (196 patches + 1 [CLS] token) and dvit =
768 is the hidden dimension of the ViT encoder.
We then pass these visual features through a linear projec-
tion layer to ensure that they are consistent with the hidden size
of our text engine, so that the visual model and the language
model are indeed speaking the same mathematical language:
Hvis = ZvisWproj + bproj
(2)

where Wproj ∈Rdvit×dgpt and dgpt = 1024 is BioGPT’s
hidden size.
C. Language Model: BioGPT
We employ BioGPT solely for the system’s “brain” and
“voice.” We employ BioGPT twice: first to generate the report
and then to encode the patient’s history. If a doctor provides
BioGPT with any notes regarding a patient, BioGPT converts
the notes into tokens. We directly obtain the raw embeddings
from BioGPT’s base layer.
Htxt = Embed(Tokenize(history)) ∈RM×dgpt
(3)
where M is the number of tokens in the prompt.
D. Cross-Attention Fusion
That’s exactly where the magic happens. To enable the
image and word data to influence each other in a natural way,
we designed a special cross-attention module. We set it up
so that the newly standardized visual embeddings are used as
keys and values, while text embeddings are used as queries:
Fcross = LayerNorm

Htxt + MHA(Htxt, Hvis, Hvis)

(4)
This is followed by a position-wise feed-forward network
(FFN) with GELU activation and residual connections:
Ffused = LayerNorm

Fcross + FFN(Fcross)

(5)
The FFN consists of two linear layers with an expansion
ratio of 4×:
FFN(x) = W2 · GELU(W1x + b1) + b2
(6)
where W1 ∈Rdgpt×4dgpt and W2 ∈R4dgpt×dgpt.
With a dropout rate of 0.1, the cross-attention module uses
a total of 8 different attention heads. In this case, having
multiple attention heads is critical because it enables the model
to look at the problem from different angles at the same time,
understanding complex relationships between what it reads
and what it sees.
E. Report Generation
We then take these projected visual components and phys-
ically attach them to the cross-attention features that have
already been fused together in a single, long sequence to
initiate the actual writing process:
Hinput = [Hvis; Ffused] ∈R(N+M)×dgpt
(7)
From there, BioGPT takes the reins. It writes the report one
word at a time using beam search. Through trial and error, we
determined that the optimal parameters were a beam size of 4,
a length penalty of 1.2 to prevent it from going on and on, a
repetition penalty of 2.5 to prevent it from getting stuck in an
infinite loop, and a strict policy against repeating any 3-word
phrases. We also set a limit on the length of the output so that
it stays between 50 and 256 tokens.

B. Backend
Powering the whole thing under the hood is a Python
backend built on Flask. It handles serving up the website itself
as well as running the REST API. Here is a quick rundown
of how it works in practice:
• Model Loading: Our pre-trained ViT and BioGPT models
are simply imported directly from the HuggingFace Hub
and slotted right into our special cross-attention model as
soon as the server is turned on. With a weight of slightly
under 447 million parameters, the final checkpoint is loaded
directly into the GPU memory and is ready to go.
• Image Preprocessing: The backend automatically resizes
the image to 224 × 224 pixels and normalizes the image to
3-channel RGB before sending it to the neural network. The
colors are normalized based on standard ImageNet baselines
µ = [0.485, 0.456, 0.406], σ = [0.229, 0.224, 0.225].
• Report Parsing: A few NLP scripts are used to clean
up the text when the model is done generating it.Using
conventional markers, it clearly separates the content into
"Findings" and "Impression" sections. The improvement
module kicks in to move the identified keywords into more
professional reports if the impression seems a bit weak.

C. Frontend
On the user side (Fig. 3), we kept things extremely light and
clinical by sticking to HTML5, vanilla JavaScript, and CSS3.
The interface offers the following to the physicians:
• A prominent drag-and-drop zone with an image preview
facility for uploading the X-ray images.
• If there is any clinical history of the patient, they can quickly
input it into a dedicated text field.
• A status light indicating whether the model is currently
employing the GPU to perform calculations for numbers.
• The results, impressions, and suggestions of the model are
displayed in a split view.
• The best part is a jsPDF-enabled one-click PDF export
button that gives them a printable medical document without
having to make another server call.

V. RESULT AND ANALYSIS
A. Dataset
To test the system, we used 50 chest X-rays directly from
the IEEE-8023 COVID dataset [10] to assess how well it coped
with real-world noise. Since it contains expert annotations
for everything from COVID-19 to bacterial pneumonia and
ARDS, this specific dataset was perfect for our requirements.
We deliberately chose a very diverse set of images, ranging
from perfectly normal ones to ones that had obvious single
issues, and a couple of multi-pathology ones.
B. Evaluation Methodology
Because it is very difficult to assess computer-generated
material, we started out by simply matching keywords. In other
words, we looked to see if the computer-generated reports
were capable of using the exact clinical language that would be
expected for a given diagnosis. We considered a draft report to
be a "pass" if it included at least 40% of the target keywords.
In addition, we performed a "gut check" to ensure that the
medical terms were valid and the anatomy was correct.

VI. CONCLUSION
Our aim was to create a deep learning model that not
only had the capability to draw boundaries but could also
generate medical reports based on chest X-rays. It is very
difficult to compare our model with the previous ones because
different people use different datasets and different metrics.
But what we think is that since we didn’t attempt to reinvent
the wheel, our approach is really unique. We have been able
to achieve a level of flexibility that was unachievable by the
previous models, which used brute force concatenation, by
using domain-specific models such as Rad-dino and BioGPT
with an ingenious cross-attention mechanism [5]. Its perfect
functionality in an online application and 100% coherence rate
on our test set of 50 images is a huge achievement.
There are a few obvious steps that we need to take next.
First, we need to stress test this on large datasets such as
CheXpert or MIMIC-CXR. Furthermore, we also need to
train this to analyze not only frontal views but also lateral
views. In terms of clinical validation, we need to perform
validation trials with active radiologists and add heatmaps so
that doctors can analyze the reasoning behind the model’s
prediction. Finally, to further improve the model without
needing a supercomputer to run it, we may look into LoRA.
